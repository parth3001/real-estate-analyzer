# BRRRR 70% Rule: When It Works and When It Destroys Your Deal (2026)

The BRRRR 70% rule is the most repeated advice in real estate investing — and the most misunderstood. Follow it blindly and you'll pass on profitable deals. Ignore it completely and you'll trap your capital in a property that can't refinance enough to recover your investment.

The problem: the 70% rule was created in a 3–4% interest rate world with 80% LTV refinances. In 2026, with rates above 7% and 75% LTV being the standard ceiling, the formula needs adjustment — or you'll either overpay or walk away from deals that actually work. This guide shows you exactly when the 70% rule still holds, when it fails, and what to use instead. You'll see the updated capital recovery formula for today's lending environment and 3 real deal breakdowns showing when to follow the rule and when to break it.

## What Is the BRRRR 70% Rule?

The formula is simple:

**Maximum Purchase Price = (ARV × 70%) − Rehab Costs**

It means: never pay more than 70% of the after-repair value, minus your rehab budget. If the ARV is $300K and your rehab costs $40K, the most you should pay is ($300K × 0.70) − $40K = $170K. If the seller wants $180K, the 70% rule says walk away.

The logic makes sense on the surface. The rule assumes you'll refinance at 75–80% LTV after rehab. Buying at 70% leaves enough margin to recover your full investment through the refinance, cover closing costs on both the purchase and refi (typically 2–3% each), and preserve a small cash flow cushion.

The rule came out of the early-to-mid 2010s, when interest rates sat at 3–4%, lenders routinely offered 80% LTV cash-out refinances on investment properties, and annual appreciation was running 5–8% in most growth markets. The math was clean: buy at 70%, refi at 80%, recover 100%+ of your capital. Infinite return.

Here's why that math doesn't hold anymore. Interest rates are 7–8%, not 3–4%. Most lenders cap investment property cash-out refinances at 75% LTV, not 80%. Appreciation has moderated to 3–4% in most markets. The result: an investor following the 70% rule to the letter in 2026 might still leave 10–20% of their capital trapped in the deal — and they may have passed on stronger deals along the way because the rule said no.

## When the 70% Rule Works

The 70% rule isn't dead. There are three scenarios where it still serves you well.

### Scenario 1: High-LTV Refinance Available

If you find a lender offering 80% LTV on a cash-out refinance — rare in 2026, but still possible through portfolio lenders or credit unions — the 70% rule works exactly as designed.

Take a property with a $300K ARV, purchased for $170K with $40K in rehab. Total invested: $210K. Refinance at 80% LTV gives you a $240K loan — that's $30K more than your total investment. Capital recovery: 114%. You're in infinite return territory, you pulled all your cash out plus extra, and the 70% rule is the reason the numbers worked so cleanly.

The challenge is finding that 80% LTV lender. Most conventional and DSCR lenders won't go above 75% on investment property cash-out refinances right now. But if you have a strong banking relationship or access to a portfolio lender, this scenario still exists.

### Scenario 2: Appreciation-Heavy Markets

In markets with strong price growth — Phoenix, parts of Texas, and Southeast metros during growth cycles — the 70% rule builds in extra margin that appreciation compounds over time.

Say you buy at the 70% threshold: $170K purchase, $40K rehab, $300K ARV. At 75% LTV, your refinance gives you $225K against a $210K investment. You've recovered $225K of $210K — already solid at 107%. But even if the refi only got you to 95% recovery today, 12–18 months of 5% appreciation pushes the ARV to $315K. A new refinance at 75% LTV now yields $236K — well into infinite return territory.

The 70% rule gives you a cushion that appreciating markets amplify over time.

### Scenario 3: Conservative Investor Profile

If your priority is risk mitigation over capital efficiency, the 70% rule is your friend. You'll typically recover 85–95% of your capital (not always 100%), but you build in margin for the things that go wrong: rehab cost overruns, a lower-than-expected appraisal, or market softening during your hold period.

The 70% rule won't maximize your returns. But it will protect you from catastrophic outcomes. For investors on their first or second BRRRR deal, that protection is worth more than the extra yield from stretching the numbers.

## When the 70% Rule Destroys Your Deal

Here's where following the rule blindly costs you money — or worse, makes you walk away from deals that actually achieve the exact outcome BRRRR is designed for.

### Problem 1: You Pass on Profitable Deals

This happens constantly. A Charlotte, NC investor finds a property listed at $200K. Rehab is $30K. ARV, verified with tight comps, is $310K.

The 70% rule says the maximum purchase price is ($310K × 0.70) − $30K = $187K. The seller won't budge from $200K. The rule says walk away.

But run the actual capital recovery math: total invested is $200K + $30K = $230K. Refinance at 75% LTV on a $310K ARV = $232,500. Capital recovered: 101%. This deal achieves infinite return — every dollar comes back plus $2,500 in your pocket — and the 70% rule told you to pass on it.

Why the disconnect? Because 75% LTV on a $310K property gives you more than enough to recover a $230K investment, even though the purchase price exceeded the 70% threshold by $13K. The rule uses a fixed percentage that doesn't flex with the actual ARV-to-investment ratio.

### Problem 2: The Rule Doesn't Account for Interest Rates

The 70% rule is completely silent on financing costs. It tells you what to pay but says nothing about what the deal looks like after you refinance into a mortgage at today's rates.

Consider the same deal in two different rate environments. In 2020 at 3.5% rates: purchase at the 70% rule ($170K), refinance into a $225K loan at 3.5%, monthly payment of $1,010. With $1,800/month rent and operating expenses, cash flow is roughly $790/month.

That same deal in 2026 at 7% rates: identical purchase, same refinance loan amount, but the monthly payment jumps to $1,497. Cash flow drops to roughly $303/month — a $487 per month reduction in income from the same property purchased at the same 70% rule threshold.

The 70% rule gave you a property that cash flows nearly $500/month less purely because of the rate environment. It doesn't adapt. The rule assumes capital recovery is the only metric that matters, but a deal you can't afford to hold after the refinance is a deal that doesn't work regardless of how much capital you recovered.

### Problem 3: It Ignores Your Actual Capital Recovery Goal

The 70% rule assumes every investor wants — and needs — 100% capital recovery. But that's not always the case.

What if 85% capital recovery is acceptable to you? Maybe you're building a long-term portfolio and you're comfortable leaving $15K–20K in each deal for higher cash flow or better properties. What if you'd rather own a property in a stronger neighborhood at 88% recovery than a marginal property in a weaker area at 100% recovery?

In these scenarios, you can pay 75–80% of ARV instead of 70% and still hit your actual investment goals. The 70% rule can't make this distinction because it treats every investor and every strategy identically.

## The Updated Formula for 2026: The Capital Recovery Formula

Instead of a rigid percentage rule, use a formula that accounts for your actual lending terms and your personal capital recovery target:

**Maximum Purchase Price = (ARV × LTV%) − Rehab Costs − Closing Costs + Acceptable Capital Left in Deal**

Or simplified: figure out how much the refinance will give you (ARV × LTV%), subtract what you need to spend (rehab + closing costs), and add back however much capital you're willing to leave in the deal.

### Example 1: Targeting 100% Capital Recovery

ARV: $300K. Lender offers 75% LTV. Rehab: $40K.

Your refinance will produce $225K ($300K × 0.75). To recover 100% of your capital, your total investment can't exceed $225K. If rehab is $40K, that leaves $185K for the purchase price.

Compare that to the 70% rule's answer: ($300K × 0.70) − $40K = $170K. The capital recovery formula lets you pay $15K more and still hit infinite return — because it uses the actual LTV percentage rather than an arbitrary 70% threshold.

### Example 2: Targeting 90% Capital Recovery

Same deal: $300K ARV, 75% LTV, $40K rehab. But you're willing to leave 10% of your investment in the deal.

Your refinance still produces $225K. If you're okay recovering only 90% of your investment, you can afford to invest up to $250K total ($225K ÷ 0.90). Subtract $40K rehab: maximum purchase price is $210K.

The 70% rule said $170K. The capital recovery formula — once you define your actual goal — says $210K. That's a $40K wider buying window, opening up deals the 70% rule would have eliminated.

### Why This Formula Is Better

It accounts for your actual LTV. The difference between 75% and 80% LTV is enormous on a $300K property — $15K in refinance proceeds. The 70% rule doesn't distinguish between the two. It lets you define your own capital recovery target instead of forcing you into a 100%-or-nothing framework. And it adjusts automatically for market conditions — if rates rise and lenders tighten LTV to 70%, the formula recalibrates while the 70% rule stays static.

## Real Deal Analysis: 70% Rule vs. Capital Recovery Formula

Let's run a real deal through both frameworks and see where they diverge.

**The deal:** Memphis, TN. Purchase price $120K, rehab budget $25K, ARV verified at $180K with three tight comps. Refinance terms: 75% LTV at 7.25%.

### 70% Rule Analysis

Maximum purchase price: ($180K × 0.70) − $25K = $101K. Your offer is $120K.

**Verdict: FAIL.** The 70% rule says walk away — you're $19K above the threshold.

### Capital Recovery Analysis

Total invested: $120K + $25K = $145K. Refinance at 75% LTV: $135K. Capital recovered: $135K of $145K = 93%. Cash left in deal: $10K.

**Verdict: PASS.** 93% capital recovery is strong. You're leaving just $10K in a property that generates income, appreciates, and builds equity through mortgage paydown.

### Cash Flow Check

Monthly rent: $1,400. Refinance loan payment on $135K at 7.25%: $933/month. Operating expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance, vacancy): $420/month. Monthly cash flow: $47/month.

**Verdict: MARGINAL.** Cash flow is thin. The property covers its own expenses, but barely. Not a cash flow play — this is a wealth-building hold where appreciation and loan paydown do the heavy lifting.

### The Final Decision

The 70% rule gave a hard no. The capital recovery formula shows a deal that recovers 93% of your investment with positive (if slim) cash flow and $10K of equity that grows over time.

Whether this deal makes sense depends on your strategy. If you're doing rapid capital recycling — flipping your BRRRR capital into the next deal every 12 months — this doesn't work. You need that $10K back. If you're building a long-term portfolio and you're comfortable with $10K locked in a property that appreciates and pays down its mortgage, this is a perfectly viable deal.

The 70% rule can't make that distinction. The capital recovery formula, paired with cash flow analysis, can.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the 70% rule dead in 2026?**
No — it's still a useful quick screening tool for filtering properties before you run a full analysis. But it shouldn't be your final decision criteria. Run the capital recovery formula to see the actual numbers. The 70% rule is conservative by design, which helps you avoid bad deals but also makes you pass on marginal-to-good deals that would actually hit your investment targets.

**What if I can only get 70% LTV on my refinance?**
Then the 70% rule becomes even more conservative — you'll recover even less capital than it implies. Use the capital recovery formula with LTV set to 70% to calculate your real maximum purchase price. At 70% LTV, you may need to buy at 60–65% of ARV to recover 90% or more of your capital. The tighter the LTV, the more purchase price discipline matters.

**Can I break the 70% rule in appreciating markets?**
You can, but be careful. If you're confident the market will appreciate 5%+ in the next 12 months, paying above the 70% threshold today can work because your ARV will be higher when you refinance. That said, banking on appreciation is speculation, not disciplined underwriting. The safer approach: use today's ARV and today's comps in the formula. If the deal works with current numbers, appreciation is a bonus, not a requirement.

**What's a good capital recovery percentage?**
100% or above is infinite return territory — the ideal BRRRR outcome where you recover all capital and redeploy it immediately. 90–99% is a strong BRRRR where you leave a small amount in the deal. 80–89% is acceptable if the cash flow is strong or you're focused on long-term wealth building. Below 80% means too much capital is trapped for the BRRRR strategy to function as intended — at that point, you're effectively doing a buy-and-hold with extra steps.

**Should I always aim for 100% capital recovery?**
Not necessarily. A deal with 88% capital recovery but strong monthly cash flow and solid appreciation potential might outperform a 100% recovery deal with razor-thin margins and weak cash flow. Capital recovery determines how quickly you can recycle into the next deal; cash flow determines whether you can afford to hold the current one.
