# ARV Calculator: How to Estimate After-Repair Value Like an Appraiser (2026)

The most expensive mistake in BRRRR investing isn't underestimating rehab costs — it's overestimating ARV. A $40K ARV miscalculation means you pay too much, over-renovate, and can't refinance enough to recover your capital. The deal dies before it starts.

This guide shows you the 3 methods appraisers actually use to calculate ARV — not guesswork, not Zillow estimates, but the comp-based formula lenders rely on when they decide how much to lend you. You'll learn how to find comps, adjust for differences in size, condition, and features, and arrive at a defensible ARV number that survives an appraiser's scrutiny. By the end, you'll estimate ARV the same way professionals do — and avoid the $40K mistakes that kill otherwise solid deals.

## What Is ARV (After-Repair Value)?

ARV is the estimated market value of a property after you complete all planned renovations. It's not what the property is worth today in its current condition — it's what it will be worth once the rehab is done and the property matches or exceeds comparable homes in the neighborhood.

Lenders use ARV to determine your refinance loan amount in a BRRRR deal. Here's why that matters: say you buy a property for $150K, spend $30K on rehab, and your ARV comes in at $220K. A 75% LTV refinance gives you a $165K loan — enough to recover most of your $180K investment. But if your ARV is off by just $20K and the actual appraised value comes back at $200K, your loan drops to $150K. That $15K difference is the gap between recovering your capital and having it trapped in the deal.

Most investors use Zillow's Zestimate or Realtor.com's automated valuation model as their ARV. These are algorithms trained on sold properties — they don't account for your specific renovation scope, the quality of your finishes, or the fact that you're creating value through rehab that hasn't happened yet. They estimate current value, not post-renovation value.

The right approach: use comparable sales in the same neighborhood, adjust for differences in size, condition, and features, and target 3–5 recent comps sold within the last 6 months and within 0.5 miles of your property.

## The 3 Methods Appraisers Use to Calculate ARV

When an appraiser shows up to value your property for the refinance, they're using one of three established methods. Knowing which one applies to your deal — and how to replicate it yourself — is the difference between an ARV estimate that holds up and one that falls apart at the finish line.

### Method 1: Sales Comparison Approach (Used 90% of the Time)

This is the method that matters for virtually every single-family BRRRR deal. The appraiser finds 3–5 comparable properties that recently sold in your area, then adjusts each comp's sale price to account for differences between that property and yours.

**How it works:** Your property is a 3-bed/2-bath, 1,500 sqft home in renovated condition. You find a comp that sold for $210K — same bed/bath count, 1,450 sqft, similar condition. The comp is 50 sqft smaller than yours, so you adjust upward: 50 sqft × $100/sqft = +$5K. Adjusted comp value: $215K.

Do this for 3–5 comps, average the adjusted values, and you have a defensible ARV estimate.

### Method 2: Cost Approach

This method calculates what it would cost to rebuild the property from scratch on the same land.

**Formula:** ARV = Land Value + (Construction Cost per Sqft × Square Footage)

**Example:** $50K land value + (1,500 sqft × $150/sqft construction cost) = $275K ARV.

The cost approach is primarily used for new construction or unique properties where no comparable sales exist. It's rarely relevant for BRRRR deals — lenders and appraisers strongly prefer the sales comparison approach for existing single-family homes.

### Method 3: Income Approach

This method values the property based on the income it produces.

**Formula:** ARV = Net Operating Income ÷ Market Cap Rate

**Example:** A property generating $18K in annual NOI in a market with a 6% cap rate → ARV = $18K ÷ 0.06 = $300K.

The income approach is used for multi-family properties (2–4 units and larger) where rental income drives value. Appraisers don't use it for single-family homes — even if you're renting the property out, a single-family BRRRR will be appraised using comps, not income.

### Which Method Should You Use?

For a single-family BRRRR deal, use the sales comparison approach. It's what your appraiser will use, so your pre-purchase ARV estimate should match their methodology. For multi-family BRRRR, lean on the income approach. For new construction, the cost approach fills the gap when comps don't exist.

## How to Find and Adjust Comps (Step by Step)

This is where most investors either build a bulletproof ARV estimate or set themselves up for a $40K surprise at appraisal. The formula is simple — finding the right comps and adjusting them correctly is the skill.

### Step 1: Define Your Search Criteria

Start tight and only expand if you can't find enough comps. Location: within 0.5 miles (closer is better, especially in urban areas where values shift block by block). Property type: match yours exactly — if you're buying a single-family detached, don't use townhomes or condos as comps. Bed/bath: within one bedroom of your property, same bathroom count. Square footage: within 20% of yours — for a 1,500 sqft property, search 1,200–1,800 sqft. Sale date: last 6 months only; fresher is always better because market conditions shift. Condition: look for properties similar to your post-rehab condition, not your property's current state.

### Step 2: Find Comps Using Free Tools

You don't need MLS access to pull solid comps, though it helps. Zillow lets you search your address, click "See nearby sales," and filter by sold date, beds, and baths. Realtor.com has similar sold-search functionality. Your county assessor's website has public records of every recent sale — this is the most reliable data source because it reflects actual recorded sale prices, not listing estimates. If you have a realtor partner, ask them to pull a CMA (Comparative Market Analysis) from the MLS — it's free and gives you the most complete comp data available.

### Step 3: Adjust for Differences

No two properties are identical, which is why raw comp prices are just a starting point. Standard adjustment values vary by market, but use these as defaults unless you have local data:

Square footage adjustments run $80–120/sqft depending on market — use $100/sqft as a starting default. An extra bedroom adds $15,000–25,000. An extra bathroom adds $10,000–15,000. A garage adds $10,000–20,000 depending on size (single vs. two-car). A pool adds $5,000–15,000 in warm climates but can actually be a negative in cold-weather markets. Condition differences require the biggest judgment call — a comp in noticeably worse condition than your post-rehab property should be adjusted upward by 10–30% depending on the gap.

### Adjustment Example (Walkthrough)

Your property after rehab: 3 bed, 2 bath, 1,500 sqft, 2-car garage, renovated condition.

**Comp 1:** 3 bed, 2 bath, 1,600 sqft, no garage, sold for $215K. The comp is 100 sqft larger (adjustment: -$10K) but has no garage while yours does (adjustment: +$15K). Adjusted value: $215K − $10K + $15K = **$220K**.

**Comp 2:** 4 bed, 2 bath, 1,500 sqft, 2-car garage, sold for $240K. The comp has an extra bedroom your property doesn't (adjustment: -$20K). Adjusted value: $240K − $20K = **$220K**.

**Comp 3:** 3 bed, 2 bath, 1,450 sqft, 2-car garage, needs work, sold for $210K. The comp is 50 sqft smaller (adjustment: +$5K) and needs roughly $20K in work to match your renovated condition (adjustment: +$20K). Adjusted value: $210K + $5K + $20K = **$235K**.

**Your ARV estimate:** Average of three adjusted comps = ($220K + $220K + $235K) ÷ 3 = **$225K**.

When all three adjusted comps land within $15K of each other, you have a solid estimate. When they diverge by $30K or more, your adjustments need work or your comps aren't truly comparable.

## ARV Calculator: Real Property Examples

### Example 1: Dallas, TX — Suburban BRRRR Deal

A 3-bed/2-bath, 1,650 sqft home with a 2-car garage. Purchase price: $180K. Rehab budget: $35K.

Three comps in the neighborhood: a 3/2 at 1,700 sqft sold for $265K (adjusted to $260K after sqft correction), a 3/2 at 1,600 sqft sold for $255K (adjusted to $260K), and a 4/2 at 1,650 sqft sold for $280K (adjusted to $260K after subtracting for the extra bedroom). All three comps converge on the same number — that's a high-confidence ARV.

**Calculated ARV: $260K.** At 75% LTV, the refinance loan is $195K against a total investment of $215K ($180K + $35K). Capital recovery: 91%. This is a strong BRRRR deal — you're getting nearly all your cash back and keeping a cash-flowing rental.

### Example 2: Phoenix, AZ — Fixer-Upper

A 3-bed/2-bath, 1,400 sqft home with no garage. Purchase price: $210K. Rehab budget: $25K.

Comps: a 3/2 at 1,450 sqft with a garage sold for $290K (adjusted down to $270K for the garage difference), a 3/2 at 1,400 sqft without a garage sold for $265K (no adjustment needed), and a 3/2 at 1,350 sqft without a garage sold for $260K (adjusted up to $265K for sqft).

**Calculated ARV: $267K.** Refinance at 75% LTV: $200K. Total invested: $235K. Capital recovery: 85%. Solid BRRRR with most capital recovered, but not infinite return territory — the purchase price was slightly high for the ARV.

### Example 3: Atlanta, GA — Full Gut Renovation

A 3-bed/2-bath, 1,500 sqft home with a 1-car garage. Purchase price: $140K. Rehab budget: $50K (full gut renovation — new kitchen, bathrooms, flooring, electrical, plumbing).

Comps: a 3/2 at 1,500 sqft with a 2-car garage sold for $235K (adjusted down to $225K for the extra garage bay), a 3/2 at 1,450 sqft with a 1-car garage sold for $220K (adjusted to $225K for sqft), and a 4/2 at 1,500 sqft with a 1-car garage sold for $245K (adjusted to $225K for the extra bedroom).

**Calculated ARV: $225K.** Refinance at 75% LTV: $169K. Total invested: $190K. Capital recovery: 89%. Good BRRRR deal, though the heavy rehab budget ate into the margin. If that $50K rehab had come in at $40K (which better contractor bidding might achieve), capital recovery jumps to 94%.

## The 5 Mistakes That Cost Investors $30K+

### Mistake 1: Using Zillow Zestimate as ARV

Zillow's algorithm is trained on historical sales data and property characteristics — it doesn't know you're about to gut the kitchen and add a bathroom. Its margin of error runs 10–20% in many markets, which on a $250K property means your ARV could be off by $25K–50K. Always use actual sold comps. That's what the appraiser will use, and your ARV estimate needs to match their methodology, not Zillow's.

### Mistake 2: Using Comps from 12+ Months Ago

Real estate markets move. In appreciating markets, old comps understate value (a less costly error). In declining markets — and 2026 has pockets of both — old comps overstate current values by 5–10%. A comp from last spring may reflect a market that no longer exists. Stick to sales from the last 6 months, and weight the most recent sales more heavily.

### Mistake 3: Not Adjusting for Condition Differences

This is the silent deal killer. You pull a comp that sold for $230K and assume your property will appraise similarly. But that comp was fully renovated with quartz countertops and new HVAC, while your rehab plan is cosmetic-only — paint, flooring, fixtures. The condition gap between the comp and your post-rehab property could be $20K+ in value. Always adjust comps for condition, not just size and bedroom count.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring Location Micro-Markets

A property two miles away might technically be in the same city but sit in a different school district, a different crime zone, or on the wrong side of a major highway. These micro-market boundaries create 10–15% value differences that won't show up if you're pulling comps too broadly. Keep comps within 0.5 miles and verify they're in the same school district when possible.

### Mistake 5: Over-Improving for the Neighborhood

You spend $60K on luxury finishes — waterfall countertops, custom cabinetry, designer tile — but the highest comp in the neighborhood sold for $220K. Your ARV is capped by what buyers in that neighborhood will pay, regardless of your renovation quality. The fix: check comps before designing your rehab scope. Renovate to match the top comps in the area, not exceed them. Every dollar spent above the neighborhood ceiling is a dollar you don't recover.

## ARV and the 70% Rule

The 70% rule is a quick screening formula that uses ARV to determine the maximum you should pay for a BRRRR property:

**Maximum purchase price = (ARV × 70%) − Rehab costs**

Using the Dallas example: ARV of $260K × 0.70 = $182K − $35K rehab = $147K maximum purchase price. The actual purchase was $180K — above the 70% rule threshold. That's why capital recovery was 91% instead of 100%+. The deal still works, but paying $33K over the 70% rule maximum is the reason it doesn't achieve infinite return.

The 70% rule is a quick-filter before making an offer. It ensures you leave enough margin for capital recovery, but it doesn't account for closing costs, holding costs, or financing terms. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a good ARV for BRRRR?**
There's no single "good" ARV — it depends on your purchase price and rehab budget. The key ratio: your ARV should be at least 140% of your total investment (purchase + rehab) to achieve full capital recovery at 75% LTV. If you invest $200K total, your ARV needs to be at least $267K for the refinance to return all your cash.

**How accurate are online ARV calculators?**
Automated tools like Zillow and Realtor.com carry a 10–20% margin of error. They estimate current value based on algorithms, not post-renovation value based on your specific rehab plan. Always validate with actual sold comps and manual adjustments — it's what the appraiser will do, so your estimate should mirror their process.

**How many comps do I need?**
Appraisers typically use 3–5 comps. Three is the minimum for a reliable average. If all three adjusted comps land within $10K of each other, your ARV estimate is solid. If they diverge by $30K or more, either your adjustments are off or your comps aren't truly comparable — go back and find tighter matches.

**Can I use active listings as comps?**
No. Only use sold properties. Active listings reflect asking prices, not market values. Sellers overprice constantly — what matters is what buyers actually paid at closing. Pending sales can serve as directional indicators, but only closed sales should feed your ARV calculation.

**What if there are no recent comps in my neighborhood?**
Expand your search radius to 1 mile and adjust more heavily for location differences. If you still can't find comparable sales, consider whether the property is too unique for a BRRRR strategy — lenders struggle to appraise properties without comparable sales data, which means your refinance could come in well below your estimate.

**Should I get a pre-appraisal before buying?**
For deals where you're stretching on purchase price or the ARV is uncertain, a pre-appraisal costs $300–500 and can save you from a $40K mistake. Most experienced BRRRR investors skip it and trust their own comp analysis, but it's cheap insurance for your first few deals while you're developing the skill.
