# Should You Pull From Your Roth IRA to Buy a Rental Property?

You've been maxing out your Roth IRA for years. The balance is sitting at $85K, maybe $120K. You're scrolling Zillow, running numbers on rental properties, and the thought creeps in: what if you pulled that Roth money and used it as a down payment on a rental that cash flows $300/month?

On paper, $300/month in cash flow beats watching your Roth compound at 8-10% annually. On paper, you're building equity, getting tax deductions, and controlling a $250K asset with $50K down. On paper, it's not even close.

The problem is that rental properties don't perform on paper. They perform in the real world — where tenants leave, water heaters explode, and that $300/month in "cash flow" quietly becomes $90 after you account for everything you forgot to budget for. Meanwhile, your Roth was compounding tax-free with zero phone calls from tenants at 11 PM.

This guide runs the actual math — not the optimistic version, the real version — so you can see exactly when pulling from a Roth makes sense, when it doesn't, and why "pretty good" rental deal isn't good enough to sacrifice tax-free compounding.

## The Scenario: A Real Investor's Dilemma

Here's the situation thousands of investors face every year. You're 32, employed, maxing your Roth IRA, and your account has grown to $95K. You've found a rental property you're excited about:

Purchase price: $220,000. Down payment: $44,000 (20%). Closing costs: $6,000. Total cash needed: $50,000. Listed rent: $1,800/month.

You don't have $50K in liquid savings outside your Roth. You could wait 18-24 months to save it, or you could pull your Roth contributions (which come out tax- and penalty-free) and buy now. The deal won't wait.

Your quick math says: $1,800 rent minus $1,170 mortgage (P&I on $176K at 7%) minus $300 in taxes and insurance = $330/month cash flow. That's $3,960/year on a $50K investment — a 7.9% cash-on-cash return. Your Roth averaged 9% last year. It feels close enough, and you get a hard asset with leverage and appreciation on top.

This is the moment where most investors make the decision based on incomplete math. Let's fix that.

## The Common Mistake: Using Gross Cash Flow

The $330/month cash flow number above is a lie. Not intentionally — it's just incomplete. It subtracts the mortgage and the two most obvious expenses (taxes and insurance) and calls whatever's left "cash flow."

Here's what it doesn't include: vacancy, maintenance, capital expenditures, property management, tenant turnover costs, and miscellaneous expenses like lawn care, pest control, or landlord-paid utilities. These aren't edge cases. They're guaranteed costs that every rental property incurs — the only question is when, not if.

When investors compare this inflated cash flow number against their Roth's 8-10% annual return and conclude the rental wins, they're comparing a real number (Roth returns) against a fantasy number (gross cash flow). The Roth's return is net — there are no hidden costs eating into it. Your rental's return needs to be net too, or the comparison is meaningless.

## Realistic Cash Flow Breakdown

Let's rebuild the cash flow on that $220K property with $1,800/month rent, this time accounting for every real cost.

**Vacancy: 7% ($126/month).** Your property will not be occupied 12 months a year, every year, forever. Tenants leave. Turns take 2-4 weeks. Even in strong rental markets, budgeting 7% vacancy means roughly 3.5 weeks per year of lost rent. In weaker markets or with older properties, 10% is more realistic.

**Maintenance and CapEx: 10% ($180/month).** This covers two categories. Routine maintenance — plumbing calls, appliance repairs, HVAC servicing, minor fixes — runs 5-6% of rent. CapEx reserves for major replacements — roof every 20-25 years ($10K-15K), HVAC every 15 years ($6K-8K), water heater every 10 years ($1.5K), appliances, flooring, exterior paint — add another 4-5%. Combined, 10% of rent is a responsible budget. First-year investors who budget 3-5% learn the real number the hard way.

**Property management: 8% ($144/month).** Even if you self-manage today, include this cost. Your time has value, your situation may change, and a deal that only works because you're providing free labor isn't a strong deal — it's a side job that happens to involve a house. If you ever want to scale beyond 2-3 properties, management becomes mandatory. Budget it from day one.

**Tenant turnover costs: $100/month (amortized).** Every time a tenant leaves, you pay for cleaning ($200-400), minor repairs and touch-up paint ($300-500), listing and showing the property ($0-500 depending on whether you use an agent), and potentially a lease-up fee to your property manager (50-100% of one month's rent). If your average tenant stays 2 years, a $2,400 turnover cost amortizes to $100/month. This is the expense most investors completely forget.

**Revised monthly cash flow:**

$1,800 gross rent, minus $126 vacancy, minus $1,170 mortgage, minus $300 taxes and insurance, minus $180 maintenance/CapEx, minus $144 property management, minus $100 turnover costs = **−$220/month.**

The property is cash flow negative. The $330/month "cash flow" from the quick math became −$220/month once you accounted for the costs that are coming whether you budget for them or not.

And this isn't a bad property. This is a typical deal in a 7% interest rate environment where rents haven't caught up to the cost of carrying a mortgage.

## Side-by-Side: Roth IRA vs. Rental Property at Real Numbers

Let's compare what happens to your $50K over 10 years under four scenarios — two for the Roth and two for the rental.

### Roth IRA Scenarios

**Roth at 8% annual return (conservative):** $50,000 grows to $107,946 in 10 years. All gains are tax-free. Total gain: $57,946. You did nothing — no calls, no repairs, no tenants. The money compounds while you sleep.

**Roth at 10% annual return (historical S&P 500 average):** $50,000 grows to $129,687 in 10 years. Tax-free. Total gain: $79,687.

### Rental Property Scenarios

**Rental at optimistic cash flow ($330/month — the incomplete number):** $3,960/year × 10 years = $39,600 in total cash flow. Plus roughly $35,000 in mortgage paydown over 10 years. Plus appreciation — at 3% annually on a $220K property, that's about $75,600 in value increase over 10 years. Total return: approximately $150,200. On paper, the rental wins convincingly.

**Rental at realistic cash flow (−$220/month — the real number):** You're feeding the property $2,640/year out of pocket. Over 10 years, that's −$26,400 in cash flow. You still get $35,000 in mortgage paydown and $75,600 in appreciation. Total return: approximately $84,200. But you subsidized the property for a decade to get there, and that return isn't tax-free — capital gains, depreciation recapture, and state taxes take a cut when you sell.

At realistic numbers, the rental's total return ($84,200) is in the same neighborhood as the Roth at 8% ($107,946) — but with dramatically more risk, effort, and illiquidity. And the Roth's $107,946 is fully tax-free. The rental's $84,200 faces capital gains tax and depreciation recapture, which could reduce the after-tax return by 15-25%.

The rental only wins clearly at the optimistic cash flow number — the one that doesn't account for vacancy, maintenance, management, and turnover. Once you use real numbers, the gap narrows or disappears entirely.

## Where the Rental Property Wins

This isn't a blanket argument against rental properties. Rentals have genuine structural advantages that a Roth IRA can't replicate.

**Leverage.** You control a $220K asset with $50K. If the property appreciates 3% annually, that's $6,600/year in value growth on a $50K investment — a 13.2% return on your cash from appreciation alone. The Roth gives you dollar-for-dollar exposure. Leverage amplifies returns in both directions, but in appreciating markets, it's a powerful wealth builder.

**Mortgage paydown.** Your tenant is paying down your loan balance every month. Over 10 years, roughly $35K of your $176K mortgage gets paid down by rental income. This is forced savings that builds equity regardless of what the market does.

**Appreciation.** Real estate in growing markets (population growth, job growth, supply constraints) has historically appreciated 3-5% annually. Combined with leverage, this is where long-term rental wealth comes from — not monthly cash flow.

**Tax advantages.** Depreciation shelters rental income from taxes. Mortgage interest is deductible. At sale, you can 1031 exchange into a larger property and defer capital gains indefinitely. These benefits are real and significant for investors in higher tax brackets.

The rental wins when the deal is strong — when actual cash flow is positive after all expenses, when the market has solid fundamentals, and when you're not sacrificing a tax-advantaged account to fund a marginal deal.

## Where the Roth IRA Wins

**Zero effort.** Your Roth compounds with no phone calls, no contractors, no leaky roofs, and no eviction proceedings. The time you'd spend managing a rental has real value — especially if you're still building your career or have other income-generating priorities.

**Tax-free growth.** This is the Roth's superpower. Every dollar of gain comes out tax-free in retirement. A rental property's returns face capital gains tax, depreciation recapture (taxed at 25%), and potentially state income tax. On a $100K gain, the tax difference can be $15K-25K.

**Liquidity.** You can access your Roth contributions at any time without penalty. Selling a rental takes 30-90 days, costs 6-8% in agent commissions and closing costs, and can't happen at all if the market is down and you're underwater. If you need cash in an emergency, the Roth is there. The rental is not.

**No concentration risk.** A diversified index fund in your Roth spreads risk across thousands of companies. A single rental property concentrates your entire investment in one asset, one neighborhood, one tenant, and one local economy. If the factory in town closes or the neighborhood declines, your rental's value and rentability decline with it.

**Guaranteed compounding.** The Roth compounds every year with no interruptions. A rental can hit you with a $12K roof replacement in year 3, an eviction that costs $5K in year 5, and a 3-month vacancy in year 7. Each disruption resets your compounding clock. The Roth's clock never stops.

## The Decision Framework

Before you pull a single dollar from your Roth for a rental property, run through this checklist.

**Check your actual trailing 12-month numbers, not projections.** If you already own rentals, look at what they actually produced over the last 12 months — not what the spreadsheet said they'd produce. Include every expense: every repair call, every vacancy day, every management fee. If your existing properties average less than 8% cash-on-cash return on real numbers, a new property purchased with Roth money is unlikely to beat tax-free compounding.

**Only pull for a clearly strong deal.** The deal needs to cash flow positive at realistic expense ratios — 7-8% vacancy, 10% maintenance/CapEx, 8% management — and still produce a cash-on-cash return that meaningfully exceeds your Roth's expected return after accounting for the tax advantages you're giving up. A deal that "works" at 5% vacancy and 5% maintenance isn't a strong deal. It's a deal that works only if nothing goes wrong.

**"Pretty good" isn't good enough.** This is the critical insight most investors miss. You're not just comparing returns — you're comparing tax-free, zero-effort, fully-liquid returns against taxable, labor-intensive, illiquid returns. The rental needs to win by a wide margin to justify the trade. A deal that produces 9% cash-on-cash when your Roth averages 9% is not a comparable return — the Roth's 9% is tax-free, effortless, and liquid. The rental's 9% is none of those things.

**Consider the opportunity cost of time.** The hours you spend finding, analyzing, financing, managing, and maintaining a rental property could be spent earning income, building a business, or advancing your career. If those hours deployed elsewhere would generate more than the rental's marginal advantage over the Roth, the Roth wins even if the raw returns are similar.

**Never pull from the Roth for a negative cash flow deal.** If the property doesn't cash flow positive at conservative expense estimates in the current rate environment, you're pulling tax-free money to subsidize an asset that costs you money every month. Appreciation and equity paydown don't pay your bills — cash flow does. Wait for a deal that actually works.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use my Roth IRA to buy rental property directly?**

No, not directly. You can't purchase real estate inside a Roth IRA without setting up a self-directed IRA with a custodian, which has complex rules and restrictions. This article discusses withdrawing contributions from your Roth (penalty-free) to use as a down payment outside the IRA.

**How much can I withdraw from my Roth IRA without penalty?**

You can withdraw your direct contributions (not earnings) at any time, tax- and penalty-free. If you've contributed $50K over 10 years, you can pull that $50K out whenever you want. Earnings withdrawals before age 59½ may face taxes and penalties unless you meet specific exceptions. The 5-year rule also applies to earnings withdrawals.

**Is it smart to pull from Roth IRA for real estate?**

Only if the deal is demonstrably strong — positive cash flow at conservative expense estimates (7-8% vacancy, 10% maintenance), 10%+ cash-on-cash return, and solid market fundamentals (population growth, job growth, supply constraints). "Pretty good" deals don't justify sacrificing tax-free compounding. The rental needs to win by a wide margin to offset the loss of tax-free growth, liquidity, and zero effort.

**What is the 5-year rule for Roth IRA?**

The 5-year rule requires your Roth account to be open for at least 5 years before you can withdraw earnings tax-free (even after age 59½). Contributions can always be withdrawn penalty-free and tax-free, regardless of the 5-year rule. The clock starts on January 1 of the year you make your first Roth contribution.

## The Bottom Line

Rental properties can absolutely outperform a Roth IRA — but only when the deal is genuinely strong, the cash flow is real (not spreadsheet fiction), and you're honest about the full cost of ownership including your time. Most deals that "beat the Roth" only beat it when you use optimistic numbers, ignore management costs because you plan to self-manage, and skip the vacancy and turnover budgets.

Run the real numbers before you touch your Roth. If the deal is clearly strong — positive cash flow at conservative assumptions, 10%+ cash-on-cash return, solid market fundamentals — it might justify pulling contributions. If it's merely "pretty good," leave your Roth alone and let tax-free compounding do what it does best.
