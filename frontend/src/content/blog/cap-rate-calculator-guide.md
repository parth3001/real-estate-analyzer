## What Is Cap Rate?

**Cap rate (capitalization rate)** measures a property's annual return based purely on its operating income—ignoring financing.

### The Formula

**Cap Rate = Net Operating Income (NOI) ÷ Property Value**

Where:
- **NOI** = Annual rental income - operating expenses (property tax, insurance, maintenance, management, utilities)
- **Property Value** = Purchase price or current market value

**Cap rate is expressed as a percentage.** A 6% cap rate means the property generates 6% of its value in annual net operating income.

### Real Example

- **Property Value:** $200,000
- **Annual Rent:** $24,000 (2 bed, 1 bath in Charlotte, NC)
- **Operating Expenses:** $12,750/year
  - Property Tax: $3,000
  - Insurance: $1,200
  - Maintenance: $2,400 (10% of rent)
  - Property Management: $2,400 (10% of rent)
  - Vacancy Reserve: $2,400 (10% of rent)
  - CapEx Reserve: $1,350 ($50/month)

**NOI = $24,000 - $12,750 = $11,250**

**Cap Rate = $11,250 ÷ $200,000 = 5.625%**

### Why Cap Rate Matters

Cap rate is the **universal language** of real estate investing:
- **Banks** use it to compare loan opportunities
- **Institutional investors** (REITs, private equity) use it to evaluate acquisitions
- **Individual investors** use it to screen deals quickly

**Most importantly:** Cap rate is **financing-agnostic**. A 6% cap rate property in Dallas is directly comparable to a 6% cap rate property in Miami, regardless of how you finance either deal.

This makes cap rate the **best initial screening tool** for comparing properties across markets, price points, and strategies.

## What's a Good Cap Rate?

**There is no universal "good" cap rate.** What's excellent in Beverly Hills (4%) would be concerning in Detroit (4%).

Cap rate reflects **risk vs. return**:
- **Lower cap rates (4-6%)** = Lower risk, stable markets, newer properties, appreciation-focused
- **Higher cap rates (7-10%+)** = Higher risk, volatile markets, older properties, cash flow-focused

### Class A Properties: 4-6% Cap Rate

**Profile:**
- Built within last 10-15 years
- Excellent locations (top school districts, low crime, strong job growth)
- High-income tenants ($75K+ household income)
- Minimal deferred maintenance

**Why Lower Cap Rates?**
- Lower vacancy (5% typical vs 10%+ in Class C)
- Lower maintenance ($100/month vs $300+/month)
- Tenant stability (average 3-year tenancy vs 1-year)
- **Appreciation** is the primary return driver

**Example Markets:** Austin, TX (4.2%), Raleigh, NC (4.8%), Denver, CO (4.5%)

**Investor Profile:** Long-term buy-and-hold, prioritizing wealth building over immediate cash flow.

### Class B Properties: 5-7% Cap Rate

**Profile:**
- Built 15-30 years ago
- Good locations (decent schools, moderate crime, stable employment)
- Middle-income tenants ($45K-75K household income)
- Some deferred maintenance, but structurally sound

**Why Moderate Cap Rates?**
- Balanced vacancy (7-8%)
- Moderate maintenance ($150-200/month)
- Good tenant stability (average 2-year tenancy)
- **Both cash flow AND appreciation**

**Example Markets:** Charlotte, NC (5.6%), Indianapolis, IN (6.2%), Kansas City, MO (5.9%)

**Investor Profile:** Best of both worlds—cash flow today, equity growth tomorrow.

### Class C Properties: 7-10% Cap Rate

**Profile:**
- Built 30+ years ago
- Marginal locations (struggling schools, higher crime, declining employment)
- Lower-income tenants ($25K-45K household income)
- Significant deferred maintenance

**Why Higher Cap Rates?**
- Higher vacancy (10-15%)
- Higher maintenance ($250-400/month)
- Tenant turnover (average 1-year tenancy or less)
- **Cash flow** is the only return driver (minimal appreciation)

**Example Markets:** Memphis, TN (8.5%), Birmingham, AL (9.2%), parts of Cleveland, OH (8-10%)

**Investor Profile:** Experienced operators who can manage higher tenant turnover and maintenance issues.

### The Cap Rate Trap

**Just because a property has a high cap rate doesn't mean it's a good deal.**

A 10% cap rate in a declining market might mean:
- Vacancy is actually 20% (not the 10% you budgeted)
- Maintenance costs $500/month (not the $200/month you budgeted)
- Rents are falling 2-3% per year
- Property value is declining

**Always validate cap rate with realistic expense assumptions.**

## Cap Rate vs. Cash-on-Cash Return

This is where most investors get confused. **Cap rate and cash-on-cash return measure completely different things.**

### Cap Rate (Financing-Agnostic)

**Formula:** NOI ÷ Property Value

**Ignores:** Mortgage, down payment, interest rate, loan term

**Use Case:** Comparing properties across markets, screening deals quickly, understanding property performance

**Example:** A $200K property with $11,250 NOI has a 5.625% cap rate **regardless of how you finance it** (all cash, 20% down, 3% down, etc.).

### Cash-on-Cash Return (Financing-Specific)

**Formula:** Annual Cash Flow ÷ Cash Invested

**Includes:** Mortgage payment (principal + interest), actual cash flow after debt service

**Use Case:** Evaluating YOUR personal return based on YOUR financing

**Example (Same $200K Property):**

**Scenario 1: All Cash**
- Cash Invested: $200,000
- Annual Cash Flow: $11,250 (same as NOI, no mortgage)
- Cash-on-Cash Return: $11,250 ÷ $200,000 = **5.625%** (matches cap rate)

**Scenario 2: 20% Down, 7% Interest, 30-Year Mortgage**
- Cash Invested: $40,000 (down payment) + $8,000 (closing costs) = $48,000
- Annual Mortgage Payment: $12,769 ($1,064/month on $160K loan)
- Annual Cash Flow: $11,250 (NOI) - $12,769 (mortgage) = **-$1,519** (negative!)
- Cash-on-Cash Return: -$1,519 ÷ $48,000 = **-3.2%** (losing money)

**Same property. 5.625% cap rate. But cash-on-cash return is -3.2% because the mortgage eats all the cash flow.**

### When to Use Each Metric

**Use Cap Rate:**
- Screening properties across different markets
- Comparing properties with different price points
- Evaluating if a property is priced correctly for its income
- Talking to commercial real estate investors (they only speak cap rate)

**Use Cash-on-Cash Return:**
- Deciding if YOU should buy this specific property with YOUR financing
- Comparing deals with different down payment requirements
- Evaluating if leverage improves your returns
- Calculating breakeven financing terms

**Pro Tip:** Always calculate BOTH. Cap rate tells you if the property is a good asset. Cash-on-cash tells you if it's a good deal for you.

## How to Calculate Cap Rate: 3 Real Examples

Let's calculate cap rate for three properties across different asset classes. All examples use **realistic expense assumptions** validated by property managers in each market.

### Example 1: Class A Property — Dallas, TX

**Property Details:**
- **Purchase Price:** $350,000
- **Property Type:** 3 bed, 2 bath, built 2018, excellent school district
- **Annual Gross Rent:** $33,600 ($2,800/month)

**Operating Expenses (Annual):**
- **Property Tax:** $7,000 (2.0% of value—TX has high property tax)
- **Insurance:** $1,800
- **Maintenance:** $3,360 (10% of rent—newer property, lower maintenance)
- **Property Management:** $3,360 (10% of rent)
- **Vacancy Reserve:** $1,680 (5% of rent—Class A has lower vacancy)
- **CapEx Reserve:** $1,200 ($100/month—newer property, lower CapEx needs)

**Total Operating Expenses:** $18,400

**NOI Calculation:**
- Gross Rent: $33,600
- Operating Expenses: $18,400
- **NOI:** $33,600 - $18,400 = **$15,200**

**Cap Rate:**
- $15,200 ÷ $350,000 = **4.34%**

**Analysis:** This is a typical Class A cap rate. The property won't cash flow much (or at all) with financing, but the bet is on **appreciation** in a high-growth market like Dallas. Good for long-term wealth building, not immediate cash flow.

### Example 3: Class C Property — Memphis, TN

**Property Details:**
- **Purchase Price:** $120,000
- **Property Type:** 3 bed, 1 bath, built 1975, lower-income neighborhood
- **Annual Gross Rent:** $14,400 ($1,200/month)

**Operating Expenses (Annual):**
- **Property Tax:** $1,800 (1.5% of value)
- **Insurance:** $1,200
- **Maintenance:** $2,880 (20% of rent—older property, higher maintenance)
- **Property Management:** $1,440 (10% of rent)
- **Vacancy Reserve:** $2,160 (15% of rent—Class C has higher vacancy)
- **CapEx Reserve:** $2,400 ($200/month—older property, frequent repairs)
- **Eviction Reserve:** $600 ($50/month—Class C has higher eviction risk)

**Total Operating Expenses:** $12,480

**NOI Calculation:**
- Gross Rent: $14,400
- Operating Expenses: $12,480
- **NOI:** $14,400 - $12,480 = **$1,920**

**Cap Rate:**
- $1,920 ÷ $120,000 = **1.6%**

**Analysis:** This is the **cap rate trap** in action. On paper, this looks like a "high cap rate market" (Memphis is often quoted at 8-10%). But when you calculate expenses **honestly**, the cap rate collapses to 1.6%.

**Why so low?**
- Maintenance is 20% (not 10%) because tenants are harder on the property
- Vacancy is 15% (not 5-10%) because tenant turnover is high
- CapEx is $200/month (not $100) because the property is old and things break
- You need an eviction reserve because evictions happen more frequently

**Key Lesson:** Don't assume "Class C = high cap rate." Class C **can** have high cap rates, but only if you're **honest about expenses**. Many investors underestimate expenses and end up with negative cash flow.

## 5 Common Cap Rate Mistakes That Cost Investors $30K+

### Mistake 1: Using Gross Rent Instead of Net Operating Income

**Wrong Formula:** Gross Rent ÷ Property Value

**Correct Formula:** NOI ÷ Property Value

**Example:**
- Property: $200,000
- Gross Rent: $24,000/year
- Wrong Cap Rate: $24,000 ÷ $200,000 = **12%** (sounds amazing!)
- Operating Expenses: $13,200
- NOI: $24,000 - $13,200 = $10,800
- Correct Cap Rate: $10,800 ÷ $200,000 = **5.4%** (reality check)

**Why This Matters:** A 12% cap rate would justify a $200K purchase price. A 5.4% cap rate means you're overpaying by $40K-60K if you assume 12%.

### Mistake 2: Forgetting Vacancy and CapEx Reserves

**Incomplete Operating Expenses:**
- Property Tax: $3,000
- Insurance: $1,200
- Maintenance: $2,400
- Property Management: $2,400
- **Total:** $9,000

**Complete Operating Expenses:**
- Property Tax: $3,000
- Insurance: $1,200
- Maintenance: $2,400
- Property Management: $2,400
- **Vacancy Reserve: $2,400**
- **CapEx Reserve: $1,800**
- **Total:** $13,200

**Impact:** Forgetting $4,200 in annual reserves overstates NOI by $4,200. On a $200K property, that's 2.1 percentage points of cap rate error.

### Mistake 3: Including Mortgage Payments in Operating Expenses

**Wrong:** NOI = Gross Rent - Operating Expenses - Mortgage Payment

**Correct:** NOI = Gross Rent - Operating Expenses (no mortgage)

**Why?** Cap rate measures the property's performance, not your financing. Mortgage is a **financing cost**, not an **operating expense**.

**Operating Expenses Include:**
- Property tax, insurance, maintenance, management, vacancy, CapEx, HOA (if applicable)

**Operating Expenses DO NOT Include:**
- Mortgage payment (principal + interest)
- Closing costs
- Down payment
- Income taxes

### Mistake 4: Comparing Cap Rates Across Different Property Classes

**Flawed Logic:** "This Class A property has a 4.5% cap rate, but this Class C property has an 8% cap rate. The Class C property is a better deal!"

**Reality:** You're comparing apples to oranges.

**Class A (4.5% cap rate):**
- Vacancy: 5%
- Maintenance: $100/month
- Tenant turnover: Every 3 years
- Appreciation: 4%/year
- **Total annual return: 8.5%** (4.5% cash + 4% appreciation)

**Class C (8% cap rate):**
- Vacancy: 15%
- Maintenance: $300/month
- Tenant turnover: Every year
- Appreciation: 0%/year
- **Total annual return: 8%** (8% cash + 0% appreciation)

**Key Lesson:** Cap rate is just ONE component of total return. Class A properties often outperform Class C properties on **total return** even though they have lower cap rates.

### Mistake 5: Ignoring Market Cap Rate Trends

**Scenario:** You buy a property in 2023 with a 6% cap rate when the market average is 5.5%.

**2026 Market Shift:** Interest rates rise, investors demand higher returns, market cap rate rises to 7%.

**Your Property's Value:**
- **2023:** $200,000 (6% cap rate, $12,000 NOI)
- **2026:** $171,429 (7% cap rate, $12,000 NOI)
- **Loss:** $28,571 (14.3% decline in value)

**Key Lesson:** Cap rates are **inversely correlated with property values**. When cap rates rise, property values fall (and vice versa). Monitor market cap rate trends to understand when you're buying high or low.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What's a good cap rate for a rental property?**

A: It depends on property class and market. Class A properties: 4-6%. Class B properties: 5-7%. Class C properties: 7-10%. But "good" is relative—a 4% cap rate in San Francisco might outperform a 9% cap rate in Detroit on total return (cash flow + appreciation).

**Q: How do you calculate cap rate?**

A: Cap Rate = Net Operating Income (NOI) ÷ Property Value. NOI = Gross Rent - Operating Expenses. Operating expenses include property tax, insurance, maintenance, management, vacancy, and CapEx reserves (but NOT mortgage payments).

**Q: Is cap rate the same as ROI?**

A: No. Cap rate ignores financing and measures property performance. ROI (or cash-on-cash return) includes your mortgage and measures YOUR return based on YOUR cash invested. A 6% cap rate property might have a 12% cash-on-cash return with leverage.

**Q: Can cap rate be negative?**

A: Yes. If operating expenses exceed rental income, NOI is negative and cap rate will be negative. This means the property loses money before even paying the mortgage. Avoid these deals unless you have a clear plan to increase income or reduce expenses.

**Q: What is a cap rate compression?**

A: Cap rate compression happens when cap rates decline (e.g., from 6% to 5%), causing property values to rise. Example: A property with $12,000 NOI is worth $200,000 at a 6% cap rate, but $240,000 at a 5% cap rate. Cap rate compression creates appreciation, but also increases buyer competition.

**Q: Should I buy a property with a low cap rate?**

A: Maybe. Low cap rates (4-6%) are common in high-growth, low-risk markets. If you prioritize **appreciation** over cash flow, a low cap rate Class A property in Austin might be a better investment than a high cap rate Class C property in a declining market.
